CANINE PERSONAL TRAINING PHILOSOPHY

Purpose, clarity, and partnership
The CPT Canine system is built around clear communication, motivation, and real-world application. A dog does not need to be punished into compliance; it needs to understand the task, understand the handler, and feel safe enough to perform under pressure.

We train the dog and the handler together. The goal is not obedience for obedience's sake, but a calm, responsive partnership that holds up in the home, on the street, and in everyday life.

Quality over chaos

Progress is built through calm repetition, not intense confusion. We teach one clear action at a time, using consistent markers and reward timing.

Motivation before pressure

Every skill starts with the dog wanting to work. We build drive, engagement, and understanding before adding distractions or difficulty.

Assessment is constant

We always review both the dog's current capacity and the handler's mechanics. If the dog is overwhelmed, we lower the challenge. If the handler is rushed, we slow the process.

Real-life reliability

The system is designed for homes, streets, parks, cafes, and public environments. We train in conditions that matter to everyday dog ownership.

The 5 CPT Classical Pillars

Pillar Mechanism Application
1. Movement & S-Flow Body mechanics, smooth transitions, and coordinated movement around the handler. Creates the structure needed for loose-lead walking, turning, and controlled movement.
2. Reward Source Focus Food and engagement are the dog’s source of interest, without forcing eye contact. Builds relevant attention, desire, and quicker response under everyday conditions.
3. Vocals & Marker System Clear, consistent praise and marker cues such as YES, YA, GOOD, STOP, and BREAK. Helps the dog understand exactly what is correct and when it is time to switch or release.
4. Drive Ignition & Modulation Food drive, play drive, and arousal are shaped and controlled to suit the task. Transforms chaotic energy into engagement, recovery, and controlled response.
5. Grip & Positive Out Drive is channelled into play and reward exchange, then released cleanly. Improves control, confidence, and calm transitions between excitement and focus.

PHASE 1: DRIVE, HANDLING & CONTROL

Session 1 Foundations
Focus: Food Drive & Hand Magnet
We establish motivation and engagement before asking for control. The dog learns to work with the handler and stay involved in the task.
  • Food drive ignition through movement, reward timing, and repetition.
  • Magnet hand work and forward push into the handler.
  • Calm, low-distraction setup in the home environment.
Homework: short daily reps Goal: motivation & engagement
Session 2 Reward Focus
Focus: Reward Source Focus & Drive Switching
We teach the dog to stay interested in the reward source while maintaining handler connection and fluid movement.
  • Clear reward source focus without forced eye contact.
  • Marker system for correct behavior and drive switching.
  • Improved forward movement and lure direction.
Homework: daily drive-switching reps Goal: clarity & response
Session 3 Movement
Focus: S-Flow & Positioning
The dog learns to move in close relation to the handler using smooth body angles, shaping, and positioning work.
  • Smooth sit, down, stand transitions.
  • Position work shaped through movement and handling cues.
  • Contact heel positions and body awareness around the handler.
Homework: 3–4 short movement drills Goal: coordination & rhythm
Session 4 Loose Lead
Focus: Lure Fading, Emergency Down, and Street Confidence
We reduce dependency on the lure, build reliable handling, and start teaching the dog to stay calm in moving environments.
  • Fading the lure and using reward efficiency.
  • Emergency down in low-distraction settings.
  • Starting loose-lead walking in realistic environments.
Homework: controlled street walks Goal: calm leadership

Phase 2 · Drive & Recall

PHASE 2: BALL DRIVE, RECALL & FOCUS

Session 5 Drive
Focus: Ball Drive & Reward Switching
We channel prey drive and excitement into a productive, controllable relationship with the handler and reward system.
  • Ball drive ignition and chase patterns.
  • Reward switching using clear markers and release cues.
  • Returning to the handler without losing intensity.
Homework: short switch drills Goal: controlled enthusiasm
Session 6 Proofing
Focus: Distraction Proofing
We add environmental pressure in a controlled way. The dog learns to stay engaged with the handler instead of reacting to novelty.
  • Front-of-house and familiar public exposure.
  • Maintaining calm in the presence of people and movement.
  • Handler mechanics and threshold awareness.
Homework: gradual exposure reps Goal: calm response under pressure
Session 7 Recall
Focus: Magnet Recall & Long-Line Return
The dog learns that returning to the handler is exciting, rewarding, and safe, even when arousal is high.
  • High-speed recall from a controlled distance.
  • Return into front position or calm stop.
  • Positive reinforcement upon arrival.
Homework: short recall reps Goal: reliable return
Session 8 Arousal Control
Focus: Emergency Down & Arousal Regulation
We teach the dog to disengage and reset when excitement rises. This is essential for public environments and impulse control.
  • Emergency down during movement and play.
  • Call-off and re-engagement under pressure.
  • Patterns that preserve emotional control.
Homework: a few calm reset drills Goal: emotional regulation

PHASE 3: SOCIALISATION, CALM & GRADUATION

Session 9 Neutrality
Focus: Exposure & Calm Socialisation
We teach the dog to remain settled around people, places, sounds, and dogs without overreacting or fixating.
  • Calm walking through shared public spaces.
  • Neutral handling around other dogs and people.
  • Stationing and settling in outdoor social environments.
Homework: calm public exposure Goal: comfort & neutrality
Session 10 Graduation
Focus: Public Field Test
This is a practical evaluation of the dog and handler working as a true team under real-life conditions.
  • Loose leash performance in public.
  • Returning focus after distractions.
  • Calm settling and handling confidence in everyday settings.
Evaluation: field walkthrough Goal: reliable partnership

CPT Handler Standards

1. Say it once, then guide clearly. Repetition creates uncertainty. Use calm body language and steady mechanics.

2. Assess both ends of the leash. If the dog fails, ask whether the task is too difficult, the timing is off, or the handler is rushing.

3. Stay calm. A relaxed handler produces a more confident and responsive dog.

4. Train the real environment. Practice should match the world the dog actually lives in.
